# How a Software Company's Accounting Books Build Up — From Day 1 to Quarter-End Financial Statements

We will start from the very beginning and follow a hypothetical Indian software company through its first quarter, from 1 April to 30 June 2026.

For **every transaction**, we will show the accounting records in the same top-to-bottom sequence:

1. **Business transaction** — what happened and what supporting document exists.
2. **Journal entry** — the debit and credit recorded.
3. **General ledger postings** — how the entry updates each affected account, including its running balance.
4. **Trial balance** — how the closing account balance appears in the consolidated list.
5. **Financial statements** — where the amount ultimately appears in the Profit and Loss Statement, Balance Sheet or Cash Flow Statement.

We will also show quarter-end adjustments and explain how the individual records combine into the final financial statements.

## 1. Our hypothetical company

**Nexora Software Private Limited**

*Illustrative Indian software company*

- **Business model:** Proprietary software products; software development and consulting services.
- **Accounting period:** 1 April–30 June 2026.
- **Currency:** Indian rupees (₹).
- **Accounting method:** Accrual accounting.

Assumptions for the example:

- The founders contribute ₹20,00,000 as share capital on 1 April.
- The company starts with no other assets or liabilities.
- Transactions are recorded through a bank account.
- The company buys equipment, pays operating expenses, develops its own software, delivers services, and sells annual software subscriptions.
- The company collects some customer payments in advance.
- We exclude Goods and Services Tax (GST), income tax, withholding taxes and statutory deductions to keep the accounting mechanics clear. These would need to be incorporated in a real company's books.
- All figures below are illustrative. Account codes are examples, and the company's accounting policies determine the final treatment of individual transactions.

## 2. Understand the accounting structure first

**Business transaction**  
*Invoice, bank receipt, supplier bill or contract*

↓

**Journal**  
*Chronological record of debit and credit entries*

↓

**General ledger**  
*Separate account-by-account records and running balances*

↓

**Trial balance**  
*Consolidated list of all general-ledger closing balances*

↓

**Financial statements**  
*Profit and Loss, Balance Sheet and Cash Flow Statement*

A journal records the transaction once, with its debit and credit lines. The system then posts those lines to the relevant ledgers. At quarter-end, the closing ledger balances are extracted into the trial balance and mapped to the financial statements.

The important distinction is that **you normally record a transaction in the journal or an appropriate accounting module, not separately in every financial statement**. The subsequent records are built from those postings.

## 3. Transaction 1 — Founders introduce ₹20 lakh as share capital

**Date:** 1 April 2026

### A. Business transaction

The founders transfer ₹20,00,000 into the company's bank account in exchange for equity share capital.

Supporting documents include the bank credit, share allotment records and corporate authorisation.

### B. Journal entry

**Journal:** JV-001 — Share capital introduced

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---:|---:|
| Bank Account (1000) | ₹20,00,000 | — |
| Share Capital (3000) | — | ₹20,00,000 |
| **Total** | **₹20,00,000** | **₹20,00,000** |

Why?

- Bank is an asset. Its increase is recorded as a debit.
- Share Capital is equity. Its increase is recorded as a credit.

### C. General ledger postings

**Ledger A: Bank Account (1000)**

| Date | Description | Debit | Credit | Running balance |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 Apr | Share capital received | ₹20,00,000 | — | ₹20,00,000 Dr |

**Ledger B: Share Capital (3000)**

| Date | Description | Debit | Credit | Running balance |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 Apr | Capital introduced | — | ₹20,00,000 | ₹20,00,000 Cr |

The journal entry has updated two separate general-ledger accounts.

### D. Trial balance impact

At this stage, the relevant balances are:

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---:|---:|
| Bank Account | ₹20,00,000 | — |
| Share Capital | — | ₹20,00,000 |

### E. Financial statement impact

**Balance Sheet — immediately after the transaction**

| Item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Bank under Current Assets | ₹20,00,000 |
| Share Capital under Equity | ₹20,00,000 |

**Profit and Loss Statement:** No impact. Share capital is not revenue.

**Cash Flow Statement:** ₹20,00,000 inflow from financing activities.

## 25. The final accounting chain — all the way through

Let's return to the security deposit example to see how the same ₹1 lakh moves through the entire accounting system.

### 1 · Source document

**Lease agreement and bank payment evidence**

Confirms the company paid a refundable deposit of ₹1,00,000.

↓

### 2 · Journal

**JV-003**

- Dr Refundable Security Deposit ₹1,00,000
- Cr Bank ₹1,00,000

↓

### 3 · General ledger

**Two account balances updated**

| Account | Effect |
|---|---|
| Security Deposit | ₹1,00,000 Dr |
| Bank | Reduced by ₹1,00,000 |

↓

### 4 · Adjusted trial balance

**Refundable Security Deposit**

Appears as a debit balance of ₹1,00,000.

↓

### 5 · Financial statements

**Balance Sheet**

Refundable security deposit: ₹1,00,000 under Assets.

No immediate expense in the Profit and Loss Statement.

This is the format to apply consistently to every accounting transaction: business event, journal, ledger, trial balance and financial statement.

One final distinction: the trial balance is a consolidated snapshot of the accounts at a given date. It is not a new accounting entry. Likewise, the financial statements are prepared from the balances and supporting records; they do not require you to re-enter each transaction.

For an actual company, the final financial statements must also incorporate applicable accounting standards, taxes, statutory disclosures, material estimates and any additional adjustments identified during the closing process.

# Appendix E — Quarter-end close checklist for a real software company

The illustrative model deliberately omits several real-world processes. A finance team would normally check, as applicable:

1. Reconcile the bank ledger to bank statements, including outstanding payments, deposits in transit, charges and errors.
2. Confirm customer invoices, service delivery evidence, credit notes, collections, receivable ageing and expected credit losses.
3. Reconcile customer advances, contract liabilities, billing schedules and revenue recognised to the performance obligations actually satisfied.
4. Reconcile supplier invoices, accrued expenses, employee costs, leave/bonus liabilities and unpaid bills.
5. Review prepaid subscriptions and other prepayments against contract periods and services consumed.
6. Maintain the fixed-asset register; confirm acquisition date, available-for-use date, useful life, residual value, depreciation method and impairment indicators.
7. For capitalised software, retain project-level cost records, technical feasibility evidence, approval, resource availability, expected benefits, the date all recognition criteria were met, the available-for-use date, useful life and impairment assessment.
8. Review research versus development classification; previously expensed research expenditure must not be reinstated as an asset merely because a later project phase qualifies.
9. Calculate current and deferred tax, applicable GST, withholding taxes and statutory employee-related liabilities under the applicable facts and law.
10. Review leases, debt, guarantees, litigation, related-party balances, foreign-currency balances and other required disclosures.
11. Check cut-off around quarter-end so revenue, costs, assets and liabilities are recorded in the correct period.
12. Reconcile the final adjusted trial balance to the financial statements and retain a documented audit trail from each material line to its ledger and source records.

These steps make the distinction clear: the accounting companion teaches the mechanics; an actual close additionally requires applicable accounting standards, law, evidence, estimates, reconciliations and disclosures.
