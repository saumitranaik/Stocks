# 1. Profit & Loss (P&L)

## 1.1 Sales / Revenue

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** During 1 April–30 June 2026, Nexora recognises ₹8,00,000 of software-services revenue when services have been delivered and ₹50,000 of subscription revenue for the June service period. **Quarter revenue = ₹8,50,000.** The ₹6,00,000 cash collected for annual subscriptions is not all current-quarter revenue: ₹5,50,000 remains a contract liability at 30 June. Revenue follows performance, not simply the date cash arrives.


**Formula:** `Sales / Revenue = Selling Price × Quantity Sold`

Sales, also called Revenue, is the money a company earns from selling
its products or services. ELI5: if a company sells 1,000 products for
₹100 each, it has ₹1,00,000 of sales. Sales tell you the size and growth
of the business, but sales alone do not tell you whether the business is
profitable because the company must pay its costs.

## 1.2 Operating Expenses

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Expenses recognised for the quarter are ₹13,28,333 (₹13.2833 lakh), including salaries ₹9,00,000, utilities ₹90,000, consumed software tools ₹30,000, research ₹1,00,000, marketing ₹60,000, cloud hosting ₹1,20,000, depreciation ₹20,000 and software amortisation ₹8,333. The ₹3,00,000 qualifying development expenditure is capitalised in this illustration, so it is not included in current-quarter expenses except for ₹8,333 amortisation. This treatment depends on evidence and the applicable accounting framework.


**Formula / reconciliation:** `Operating expenses excluding D&A ≈ Revenue − EBITDA` only when EBITDA is defined as revenue less those operating expenses and no other operating items affect the calculation. Reconcile the company's own EBITDA definition; this is not a universal identity.

Operating expenses are the costs required to run the normal business,
such as raw materials, salaries, power, rent, logistics, marketing and
administration. ELI5: sales are the money coming into the shop;
operating expenses are the money needed to keep the shop running. The
key question is whether expenses are growing slower or faster than
sales.

## 1.3 EBITDA [Operating Earnings Before D&A]

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Using the illustrative classifications in the accounting companion, EBITDA = ₹8,50,000 revenue − ₹13,00,000 operating expenses before depreciation and amortisation = **loss of ₹4,50,000**. EBITDA is not cash flow; customer advances help cash generation even while EBITDA is negative.


**Formulas:**
- `EBITDA = EBIT + Depreciation + Amortisation`
- `EBITDA ≈ Revenue − Operating Expenses excluding D&A`, when the expense categories and other operating items are consistently defined.

EBITDA means Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation and Amortisation. It is an accounting-based performance measure derived from financial statements, but it is not a standardised cash-flow measure and is not defined identically by every company. Some Indian company presentations use “operating profit” to mean EBITDA; check the company's definition rather than assuming the terms are interchangeable.

**EBITDA is not cash and is not the same as accounting net profit.** It excludes depreciation and amortisation, interest and tax, and it does not directly reflect when customers pay, when suppliers are paid, working-capital movements or capital expenditure. ELI5: EBITDA helps assess operating performance before certain costs; it does not tell you how much cash is available to spend. PAT/net profit is generally what is meant by the accounting bottom line.

## 1.4 Operating Profit Margin (OPM) / EBITDA Margin

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** EBITDA margin = −₹4,50,000 ÷ ₹8,50,000 × 100 = **−52.9%**. This is a start-up quarter with a small revenue base and ₹9 lakh of salaries; do not treat it as a stable margin or compare it mechanically with mature software companies.


**Formula:** `EBITDA Margin % = EBITDA / Revenue × 100`

Use `Operating Profit Margin % = Operating Profit / Revenue × 100` only after confirming how the company defines “operating profit.” If operating profit means EBIT, the margin includes depreciation and amortisation; if it means EBITDA, it excludes them.

ELI5: a 20% EBITDA margin means the company reports ₹20 of EBITDA for every ₹100 of revenue. It does not mean ₹20 is left as cash. Compare companies only after aligning their definitions and treatment of exceptional or adjusted items.

## 1.5 Other Income

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** The worked example assumes no other income. Therefore no amount is added between operating profit and profit before tax. In real statements, inspect interest income, foreign-exchange gains and other non-operating items separately instead of automatically treating them as recurring operating performance.


**Formula:** `Total Income = Operating Revenue + Other Income`

Other Income is money earned outside the company’s primary operating
business, such as interest on investments, dividends or investment
gains. ELI5: a company can make money from selling its products and also
from temporarily investing surplus cash. Other Income can improve
reported profit, but it should normally be separated from recurring
core-business performance.

## 1.6 Depreciation

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Equipment costing ₹2,40,000 is depreciated over an assumed 36 months with no residual value. The example records ₹20,000 depreciation for the quarter, leaving net equipment of ₹2,20,000. The actual commencement date and useful life must follow the applicable policy and when the asset is available for use.


**Formula:**
`Straight-Line Depreciation ≈ (Asset Cost − Residual Value) / Useful Life`

Depreciation is the systematic allocation of the cost of a physical
long-term asset over its useful life. Examples include machinery,
factories, buildings and vehicles. ELI5: if a machine costs ₹10 lakh and
lasts 10 years, accounting spreads its cost over those years.
Depreciation reduces accounting profit, but the depreciation charge
itself is generally not a cash payment in that period.

## 1.7 Amortisation

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Capitalised software cost is ₹3,00,000. Assuming it became available for use on 1 June 2026, has a 36-month useful life and no residual value, June amortisation is ₹3,00,000 ÷ 36 = **₹8,333.33**. Net carrying amount at quarter-end is ₹2,91,666.67 before any impairment. Capitalisation itself must first be justified.


**Formula:**
`Straight-Line Amortisation ≈ (Intangible Asset Cost − Residual Value) / Useful Life`

Amortisation is broadly the equivalent of depreciation for intangible
assets such as software, patents, licences and copyrights. ELI5:
depreciation spreads the cost of something physical; amortisation
spreads the cost of something intangible. It reduces accounting profit
without normally representing a separate cash payment in the period
recorded.

## 1.8 EBIT

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** EBIT = EBITDA loss ₹4,50,000 − depreciation ₹20,000 − software amortisation ₹8,333 = **loss of ₹4,78,333**. This matches the illustrative loss before tax because the example assumes no interest, other income or income-tax expense.


**Formula:** `EBIT = EBITDA − Depreciation − Amortisation`

EBIT means Earnings Before Interest and Tax. It measures profit after
recognising depreciation and amortisation but before interest and tax.
ELI5: EBITDA asks what the operating business made before asset-use
charges; EBIT asks what remains after recognising those charges.
Therefore EBITDA and EBIT are different whenever depreciation or
amortisation exists.

## 1.9 Interest \[Finance Cost\]

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** No borrowing or interest expense is included. Interest coverage ratios are therefore not meaningfully testable from this example; zero interest in a simplified model is not evidence that a real company has no lease interest or financing costs.


**Formula:** `Interest Expense ≈ Borrowings × Applicable Interest Rate`

Interest, often called Finance Cost, is the cost of borrowing money.
ELI5: if a company borrows ₹100 crore from a bank, the bank charges it
for using that money. Interest reduces profit after financing costs, so
a company can have strong operating profit but weaker net profit if its
debt burden is high.

## 1.10 Profit Before Tax \[PBT\]

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** The model's pre-tax loss is ₹4,78,333. It assumes no other income, finance cost or exceptional item. A real financial statement may require additional accruals and adjustments before PBT is final.


**Formula:**
`PBT = EBIT + Other Income − Interest ± Other Applicable Items`

Profit Before Tax is the profit remaining after operating costs,
depreciation/amortisation, financing costs and relevant non-operating
items, but before income tax. ELI5: PBT is the company’s profit just
before calculating the government’s share.

## 1.11 Tax Expense

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Income tax is explicitly excluded from the accounting companion to teach the mechanics. Therefore the example's PBT must not be represented as a complete statutory tax computation or a real tax provision. Deferred tax, current tax and tax effects of capitalised development may need consideration.


**Formula:** `Tax Expense ≈ PBT × Effective Tax Rate`

Tax Expense is the income-tax cost recognised for the accounting period.
ELI5: if PBT is ₹100 and the effective tax rate is 25%, approximately
₹25 is recognised as tax expense. The actual accounting number can
differ because of deferred tax, incentives, deductions and other
adjustments.

## 1.12 Effective Tax Rate \[Tax %\]

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Not calculated. With tax excluded and a pre-tax loss, an effective tax rate would be misleading. In actual analysis, explain the tax charge or benefit and avoid interpreting a tax rate mechanically when PBT is negative or near zero.


**Formula:** `Effective Tax Rate = Tax Expense / PBT × 100`

The Effective Tax Rate shows what proportion of PBT is recognised as tax
expense. ELI5: if PBT is ₹100 and tax expense is ₹25, the effective rate
is 25%. It can differ from the statutory rate because of exemptions, tax
credits, deferred tax and other accounting effects.

## 1.13 Net Profit \[PAT\]

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** If one temporarily assumes no income-tax expense, the illustrative accounting loss after tax would also be ₹4,78,333. This is only a teaching simplification, not a completed statutory PAT figure because tax and other real-world adjustments are excluded.


**Formula:** `Net Profit / PAT = PBT − Tax Expense`

Net Profit, or Profit After Tax, is the bottom-line profit remaining
after major operating, financing and tax costs. ELI5: this is what is
left for the shareholders after the company has accounted for its major
expenses. Net profit should be considered together with cash flow
because accounting profit and cash generated are not identical.

## 1.14 EPS \[Earnings Per Share\]

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** EPS cannot be calculated because the example does not specify the number of equity shares or weighted-average shares. Share capital of ₹20,00,000 is a rupee amount, not a share count; do not divide profit by share capital as a substitute.


**Formula:**
`EPS = Profit Attributable to Ordinary Shareholders / Weighted-Average Ordinary Shares`

EPS translates company earnings into an amount attributable to each
ordinary share. ELI5: instead of saying the company earned ₹100 crore,
EPS asks how much of that profit corresponds to one share. EPS is
important because share prices are ultimately related to earnings
generated for each share.

# 2. Balance Sheet

## 2.1 Assets

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Total assets at 30 June 2026 are ₹23,71,667: bank ₹14,70,000; trade receivables ₹2,00,000; equipment net ₹2,20,000; software net ₹2,91,667; prepaid software tools ₹90,000; and refundable security deposit ₹1,00,000. These are carrying amounts after the stated adjustments.


**Formula:** `Total Assets = Total Liabilities + Shareholders' Equity`

Assets are resources controlled by the company that are expected to
provide future economic benefit. They include cash, inventory,
receivables, property, machinery, investments and intangible assets.
ELI5: assets are the things the company owns or controls that help it
operate or have value.

## 2.2 Non-Current Assets

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Equipment net of depreciation is ₹2,20,000 and capitalised software net of amortisation is ₹2,91,667. The ₹1,00,000 refundable deposit is classified as a non-current asset only if its expected recovery is beyond twelve months; the companion's balance sheet does not specify recovery timing, so classification should be confirmed rather than assumed.


**Formula:** `Non-Current Assets = Total Assets − Current Assets`

Non-current assets are longer-term resources, normally expected to
provide benefit beyond one year or the normal operating cycle. They
include property, plant and equipment, long-term investments and many
intangible assets. ELI5: these are the company’s longer-term resources,
such as factories and machinery.

## 2.3 Current Assets

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Current assets explicitly identifiable from the example are bank ₹14,70,000, trade receivables ₹2,00,000 and prepaid tools ₹90,000, totalling ₹17,60,000. This excludes the refundable deposit pending confirmation of its expected recovery date.


**Formula:**
`Current Assets = Cash + Receivables + Inventory + Other Short-Term Assets`

Current assets are resources expected to be converted into cash, sold or
consumed within the normal operating cycle or generally within one year.
ELI5: these are assets relatively close to becoming usable cash, such as
inventory and customer receivables.

## 2.4 Cash & Cash Equivalents

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Closing bank cash is ₹14,70,000, reconciled to opening share-capital cash plus the quarter's receipts and payments. This is not the same as profit: Nexora reports a loss while cash rises because founders contributed ₹20 lakh and customers paid advances/subscriptions.


**Formula:**
`Cash & Equivalents = Cash + Highly Liquid Short-Term Investments`

Cash and cash equivalents are immediately available or highly liquid
financial resources. ELI5: this is money the company can access quickly
to pay suppliers, employees, lenders or make investments. Cash provides
flexibility, although excessive idle cash can also indicate
under-utilised capital.

## 2.5 Inventory

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** No inventory is assumed. Software source code and internally developed software should not automatically be labelled inventory; classification depends on the nature of the asset, the business model and applicable accounting standards.


**Formula:**
`Average Inventory = (Opening Inventory + Closing Inventory) / 2`

Inventory consists of raw materials, work in progress and finished
goods. ELI5: it is stuff the company has bought or made but has not yet
converted into sales. Too little can cause lost sales; too much can trap
cash and create storage or obsolescence risk.

## 2.6 Trade Receivables \[Debtors\]

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Customer A was invoiced ₹8,00,000 for delivered services and paid ₹6,00,000, leaving ₹2,00,000 outstanding at 30 June. The receipt reduces receivables; it does not create revenue a second time.


**Formula:**
`Average Receivables = (Opening Receivables + Closing Receivables) / 2`

Trade receivables, commonly called debtors, are amounts customers owe
for goods or services already delivered. ELI5: the company has made the
sale but has not yet received the cash. Receivables growing much faster
than sales can signal slower collections or weaker earnings quality.

## 2.7 Trade Payables \[Creditors\]

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** No trade-payable balance remains because the model assumes the listed suppliers were paid. Payable days therefore cannot be calculated from this case. Real accounts must include unpaid supplier invoices and accrued expenses when goods or services have been received.


**Formula:**
`Average Payables = (Opening Payables + Closing Payables) / 2`

Trade payables, commonly called creditors, are amounts owed to suppliers
for goods or services already received. ELI5: the supplier has supplied
the goods but the company has not yet paid. Supplier credit can support
cash flow, but extreme payment delays can indicate financial stress.

## 2.8 Current Liabilities

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Contract liabilities are ₹8,50,000 in total: ₹5,50,000 for the unearned portion of annual subscriptions and ₹3,00,000 received for future implementation services. These are assumed to be current because the relevant service obligations are expected to be fulfilled within the next year.


**Formula:**
`Current Liabilities = Short-Term Obligations Due Within the Current Operating Cycle`

Current liabilities are obligations expected to be settled in the short
term, such as trade payables, short-term borrowings and accrued
expenses. ELI5: these are bills and obligations the company expects to
have to pay relatively soon.

## 2.9 Total Liabilities

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Total listed liabilities are ₹8,50,000, entirely contract liabilities in this simplified case. This does not imply that a real company has no tax, employee, lease, vendor or other liabilities; those were excluded or assumed settled.


**Formula:**
`Total Liabilities = Current Liabilities + Non-Current Liabilities`

Liabilities are obligations owed to lenders, suppliers, employees,
governments and other parties. ELI5: liabilities are claims of outsiders
against the company’s assets. Debt and other liabilities can help a
company grow, but excessive obligations increase financial risk.

## 2.10 Shareholders’ Equity \[Net Worth\]

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Share capital is ₹20,00,000 and the quarter's accumulated loss is ₹4,78,333 under the no-tax teaching assumption, giving illustrative equity of ₹15,21,667. The actual equity roll-forward would include all recognised tax, other comprehensive income, dividends and prior-period balances, if any.


**Formula:** `Shareholders' Equity = Total Assets − Total Liabilities`

Shareholders’ Equity, commonly called Net Worth, is the accounting
residual belonging to shareholders after liabilities are deducted from
assets. ELI5: if the company sold its assets and paid its liabilities,
the accounting amount left would broadly represent shareholders’ equity.

## 2.11 Net Debt

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** The model contains no interest-bearing borrowings and has ₹14,70,000 bank cash, so a simplified net-debt calculation would show net cash of ₹14,70,000, excluding any unmodelled lease liabilities or restricted cash. This is a model assumption, not a claim about a real company.


**Formula:** `Net Debt = Total Debt − Cash & Cash Equivalents`

Net Debt measures borrowings after considering cash holdings. ELI5:
owing ₹100 crore is less concerning if the company has ₹80 crore of cash
than if it has ₹5 crore. Net debt is useful for assessing leverage and
enterprise value.

## 2.12 Book Value Per Share \[BVPS\]

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** BVPS is unavailable because the number of issued equity shares is not supplied. Equity of ₹15,21,667 alone is insufficient; divide the appropriate equity attributable to ordinary shareholders by the relevant number of shares.


**Formula:**
`BVPS = Equity Attributable to Ordinary Shareholders / Number of Ordinary Shares`

Book Value Per Share shows accounting net assets attributable to each
share. ELI5: it estimates the accounting value corresponding to one
share after liabilities are deducted from assets. It is particularly

# 3. Working Capital & Operating Efficiency

## 3.1 Working Capital

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** The identifiable current assets are ₹17,60,000. Current contract liabilities are ₹8,50,000. The broad current-assets-minus-current-liabilities measure is therefore ₹9,10,000, subject to confirming classification of the refundable deposit and any omitted current balances.


**Formula:** `Working Capital = Current Assets − Current Liabilities`

Working capital measures short-term balance-sheet resources available to support operations. The broad accounting formula is `Current Assets − Current Liabilities`; this can include cash, borrowings and other items that are not part of day-to-day operating working capital. Net operating working capital is often analysed separately, using relevant operating items such as inventory and trade receivables less trade payables (and, where appropriate, other operating current assets and liabilities).

**Working capital is not Free Cash Flow (FCF).** Working capital is a balance-sheet amount at a point in time. The *change* in operating working capital over a period affects operating cash flow: an increase usually absorbs cash, while a decrease usually releases cash, all else equal. ELI5: a company may be profitable but still have cash tied up in unpaid customer bills or inventory.

## 3.2 Net Working Capital

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** A broad balance-sheet NWC proxy is ₹17,60,000 current assets − ₹8,50,000 current liabilities = ₹9,10,000. Operating NWC can be defined differently—often excluding cash and financing items—so use a consistent definition before comparing companies.


**Formula:**
`Net Working Capital = Inventory + Trade Receivables − Trade Payables`

Net Working Capital focuses on operating assets and liabilities that drive the cash cycle. The simple formula above is a useful approximation, not a universal definition; some analyses also include contract assets, other operating receivables, accrued operating liabilities and deferred revenue, depending on the business model. State which items are included and apply the definition consistently.

ELI5: it estimates how much cash is tied up in operating assets after considering supplier credit and other included operating liabilities. Efficient working-capital management can release cash without reducing operations. The *change* in this amount—not its closing balance by itself—is what affects cash flow for a period.

## 3.3 Inventory Days

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Not applicable to this example because no inventory or cost of goods sold is assumed. Do not force an inventory-days calculation onto a software-services or subscription business without an economically meaningful inventory base.


**Formula:**
`Inventory Days = Average Inventory / Cost of Goods Sold × 365`

Inventory Days estimate how long inventory remains in the business
before being sold or consumed. ELI5: 60 inventory days means inventory
is tied up for roughly two months on average. The appropriate level
varies by industry and business model.

## 3.4 Receivable Days \[Debtor Days\]

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** A rough first-quarter estimate using average receivables is (₹0 opening + ₹2,00,000 closing) ÷ 2 = ₹1,00,000 average receivables. Dividing by ₹8,00,000 of credit services revenue and multiplying by 91 days gives **about 11.4 days**. This is a weak estimate for a start-up with one invoice; use consistent credit-sales data and longer periods for meaningful analysis.


**Formula:**
`Receivable Days = Average Trade Receivables / Credit Sales × 365`

Receivable Days estimate how long customers take to pay. ELI5: 45 days
means the company is waiting roughly a month and a half to collect its
sales. Lower days generally mean faster cash collection, all else equal.

## 3.5 Payable Days \[Creditor Days\]

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Not calculable because the example has no closing trade payables and does not provide credit purchases. A zero payable balance should not be converted into a claim of exceptional payment efficiency.


**Formula:**
`Payable Days = Average Trade Payables / Credit Purchases × 365`

Payable Days estimate how long the company takes to pay suppliers. ELI5:
60 days means the company is using supplier credit for roughly two
months. Higher payable days can support cash flow, but very high levels
can indicate payment pressure.

## 3.6 Cash Conversion Cycle \[CCC\]

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Not meaningful from the available data: no inventory cycle is assumed, payable days are unavailable, and this is a short start-up quarter. For software businesses, focus additionally on receivable days, contract assets/liabilities and the timing of customer prepayments.


**Formula:** `CCC = Inventory Days + Receivable Days − Payable Days`

The Cash Conversion Cycle estimates how long cash remains tied up
between paying for inputs and collecting cash from customers. ELI5: the
company buys materials, makes and sells products, and waits for
customers to pay. A shorter CCC generally means cash comes back faster.

# 4. Cash Flow

## 4.1 Operating Cash Flow \[CFO\]

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** CFO under the indirect method is ₹1,10,000. The simplified bridge is pre-tax loss −₹4,78,333 + depreciation/amortisation ₹28,333 − increase in receivables ₹2,00,000 − increase in prepayments ₹90,000 + increase in contract liabilities ₹8,50,000 = ₹1,10,000. The positive CFO is helped by cash received before revenue recognition.


**Formula:**
`CFO ≈ Net Profit + Non-Cash Expenses − Non-Cash Gains ± Working Capital Changes`

Operating Cash Flow measures cash generated or consumed by normal
business operations. ELI5: profit is an accounting measure; CFO asks
whether the everyday business actually produced cash. A healthy business
generally needs to convert a meaningful portion of profit into operating
cash over time.

## 4.2 Investing Cash Flow

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Investing cash flow is −₹6,40,000: equipment purchase −₹2,40,000, capitalised development −₹3,00,000 and refundable deposit −₹1,00,000, using the classifications assumed in the companion. Deposit classification must follow its nature and the applicable cash-flow policy.


**Formula:**
`Investing Cash Flow = Cash Received from Investments − Cash Spent on Investments`

Investing Cash Flow records cash movements related to long-term assets
and investments, such as buying or selling property, plant and equipment
or investments. ELI5: it shows whether the company is spending cash to
build the future business or receiving cash from selling assets and
investments.

## 4.3 Financing Cash Flow

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Financing cash flow is +₹20,00,000 from founders' share capital. There are no borrowings, repayments, dividends or buybacks in the model.


**Formula:**
`Financing Cash Flow = Debt Raised − Debt Repaid + Equity Raised − Dividends/Buybacks ± Other Financing Items`

Financing Cash Flow shows cash moving between the company and lenders or
shareholders. ELI5: it tells you whether the company is borrowing,
repaying debt, raising equity, paying dividends or buying back shares.

## 4.4 Capital Expenditure \[Capex\]

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Physical equipment Capex is ₹2,40,000. If an analytical Capex measure includes capitalised software development, total capital investment is ₹5,40,000. State which definition is used; the refundable deposit is shown separately in the example.


**Formula:**
`Capex = Cash Spent on Acquiring or Improving Long-Term Operating Assets`

Capital Expenditure is money spent on assets that provide benefits over
multiple years, such as factories, machinery, technology and equipment.
ELI5: buying a machine that will help the company produce for years is
Capex. Some Capex maintains the existing business; other Capex expands
it.

## 4.5 Free Cash Flow [FCF]

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Using FCF = CFO − physical equipment Capex − capitalised software development cash spend, FCF = ₹1,10,000 − ₹2,40,000 − ₹3,00,000 = **−₹4,30,000**. This analytical definition excludes the separately classified security deposit and should be labelled consistently. FCF is negative despite positive CFO because Nexora is investing in equipment and software.


**Common formula:** `FCF = Operating Cash Flow (CFO) − Capital Expenditure (Capex)`

This is a widely used FCF definition and, because CFO generally reflects interest and taxes paid under the applicable cash-flow presentation, it is often closer to levered/free cash flow to equity than to unlevered free cash flow. Companies and analysts may use different definitions. State whether Capex includes purchases of property, plant and equipment and intangible assets, and whether any disposals are netted against it. Do not silently compare differently defined FCF figures.

FCF is a cash-flow measure over a period; working capital is a balance-sheet measure at a point in time. Changes in operating working capital affect CFO and therefore FCF, but working capital itself is not FCF. ELI5: if CFO is ₹100 crore and Capex is ₹30 crore, FCF is approximately ₹70 crore under this definition. The cash may then be used for debt repayment, dividends, buybacks, acquisitions or cash reserves; it is not automatically all distributable to shareholders.

## 4.6 EBITDA-to-Cash Bridge (Illustrative)

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** EBITDA is −₹4,50,000 while CFO is +₹1,10,000. The gap is not an arithmetic contradiction: CFO reflects working-capital timing, including ₹8,50,000 of contract liabilities, plus cash-flow classification and other adjustments. Reconcile the full bridge rather than treating EBITDA as cash.


EBITDA should not be treated as cash generated. A simplified bridge can help show why:

| Item | Illustrative amount (₹ crore) |
|---|---:|
| EBITDA | 40 |
| Less: cash interest paid | (5) |
| Less: cash taxes paid | (7) |
| Less: increase in operating working capital | (12) |
| **Illustrative operating cash remaining** | **16** |
| Less: Capex paid | (15) |
| **Illustrative FCF** | **1** |

This illustration assumes no other cash-flow adjustments, and is not a substitute for the reported cash-flow statement. In actual accounts, CFO is reconciled using the applicable accounting presentation; working-capital changes, non-cash items, interest and tax classification can vary. The example demonstrates that ₹40 crore of EBITDA can coexist with only ₹1 crore of FCF.

## 4.7 Cash Conversion

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** CFO/PAT is not a useful ratio because the teaching case has a loss and excludes tax; the denominator is negative. Instead, examine the absolute CFO bridge, receivables, prepayments, contract liabilities and investment outflows.


**Formula:** `Cash Conversion = Operating Cash Flow / Net Profit × 100`

Cash Conversion measures how effectively accounting profit becomes
operating cash. ELI5: if the company reports ₹100 of profit but
generates ₹40 of operating cash, only 40% of reported profit converted
to operating cash in that period. One year can be distorted by
working-capital movements, so trends matter.

# 5. Returns & Capital Efficiency

## 5.1 ROE \[Return on Equity\]

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Not suitable as a performance measure for this start-up quarter without careful annualisation and a consistent average-equity basis. Equity changed from the founders' contribution as the quarter's loss accrued; use the full-period average equity and comparable periods before drawing conclusions.


**Formula:** `ROE = Net Profit / Average Shareholders' Equity × 100`

ROE measures how much profit a company generates from shareholders’
accounting capital. ELI5: if shareholders have ₹100 invested and the
company earns ₹15, ROE is about 15%. High ROE can indicate efficiency,
but debt can magnify ROE, so it should be considered with leverage and
ROCE.

## 5.2 ROCE \[Return on Capital Employed\]

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Not interpreted as a mature-company return because Nexora has a one-quarter operating history, negative EBIT and a newly funded balance sheet. A negative quarterly EBIT divided by a capital base can be computed only after defining capital employed and annualisation; it should not be compared directly with annual peer ROCE.


**Formula:**
`ROCE = EBIT / Average Capital Employed × 100 Capital Employed ≈ Total Assets − Current Liabilities`

ROCE measures how efficiently the operating business uses capital
committed to it. ELI5: it asks how much operating profit the company
generates from the money tied up in the business. It is especially
useful for capital-intensive companies because it considers debt- and
equity-funded capital.

## 5.3 ROA \[Return on Assets\]

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Not a reliable stand-alone signal for this single quarter. If calculated, use period EBIT or PAT as the numerator consistently and average assets as the denominator; explain whether the quarter is annualised.


**Formula:** `ROA = Net Profit / Average Total Assets × 100`

ROA measures how efficiently the company uses its asset base to generate
profit. ELI5: if ₹100 of assets generate ₹10 of net profit, ROA is about
10%. Asset-heavy businesses naturally tend to have lower ROA than
asset-light businesses.

## 5.4 Asset Turnover

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Use revenue of ₹8,50,000 and average assets if opening and closing balances are available. The company started with no assets, so a naïve ratio is heavily affected by the timing of the capital contribution and purchases and should not be compared with mature peers.


**Formula:** `Asset Turnover = Sales / Average Total Assets`

Asset Turnover measures how much sales the company generates from its
assets. ELI5: if ₹100 of assets generate ₹200 of sales, asset turnover
is 2×. A company can improve returns through higher margins, faster
asset turnover, or both.

# 6. Growth & Earnings Quality

## 6.1 Revenue Growth

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Not calculable because the case contains only the first quarter and no comparable prior-year or prior-quarter baseline. Revenue for the quarter is ₹8,50,000, but a single amount is not a growth rate.


**Formula:**
`Revenue Growth % = (Current Revenue − Previous Revenue) / Previous Revenue × 100`

Revenue Growth measures how quickly sales are increasing. ELI5: sales
rising from ₹100 to ₹120 means 20% growth. Sustainable growth is
generally more valuable than growth caused by temporary price changes,
acquisitions or one-off events.

## 6.2 EBITDA Growth

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Not calculable without a comparable prior period. Current-quarter EBITDA is a loss of ₹4,50,000; percentage growth formulas can be misleading when the base is negative or crosses zero.


**Formula:**
`EBITDA Growth % = (Current EBITDA − Previous EBITDA) / Previous EBITDA × 100`

EBITDA Growth measures growth in operating profit before interest, tax,
depreciation and amortisation. ELI5: it tells you whether the core
business is generating more operating profit. Comparing EBITDA growth
with revenue growth can reveal operating leverage or margin pressure.

## 6.3 EBIT Growth

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Not calculable without comparative data. Current-quarter EBIT is a loss of ₹4,78,333 under the stated assumptions.


**Formula:**
`EBIT Growth % = (Current EBIT − Previous EBIT) / Previous EBIT × 100`

EBIT Growth measures growth in operating profit after depreciation and
amortisation. ELI5: it tells you whether the business is becoming more
profitable even after recognising the cost of using its long-term
assets.

## 6.4 Net Profit Growth

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Not calculable. The model does not include comparative periods or a complete tax provision; use absolute profit/loss movement and explain the accounting basis instead of applying a misleading growth percentage.


**Formula:**
`Net Profit Growth % = (Current PAT − Previous PAT) / Previous PAT × 100`

Net Profit Growth measures how quickly bottom-line earnings are
increasing. ELI5: it tells you whether the amount ultimately earned for
shareholders is growing. It is influenced by operations, interest, other
income, depreciation, tax and exceptional items.

## 6.5 EPS Growth

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Not calculable because neither EPS nor the weighted-average share count is specified and no comparative period is available.


**Formula:**
`EPS Growth % = (Current EPS − Previous EPS) / Previous EPS × 100`

EPS Growth measures the increase in earnings attributable to each share.
ELI5: a company may increase total profit but issue many new shares,
causing profit per share to grow more slowly. EPS therefore connects
company-level profit growth with each shareholder’s economic interest.

## 6.6 CAGR \[Compound Annual Growth Rate\]

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Not calculable from a single quarter. CAGR requires a beginning value, ending value and multi-year time interval, with care when values are zero or negative.


**Formula:** `CAGR = (Ending Value / Beginning Value)^(1/n) − 1`

CAGR shows the annualised rate at which a value would have grown if it
had increased at a constant compounded rate. ELI5: it turns a multi-year
start-to-end change into one equivalent annual growth rate. It smooths
the path and therefore does not show year-to-year volatility.

## 6.7 Earnings Quality

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** The quarter has a ₹4.78 lakh illustrative pre-tax loss but ₹1.10 lakh positive CFO, driven largely by customer cash advances and unearned subscription receipts. This is a useful example of why earnings quality analysis must reconcile profit to cash and inspect contract liabilities—not simply label positive CFO as proof of strong economics.


**Formula:** `Cash Conversion = Operating Cash Flow / Net Profit`

Earnings Quality asks whether reported accounting profit represents
strong, sustainable economic performance and cash generation. ELI5: if a
company says it made ₹100 of profit, you want to know whether customers
actually paid, receivables are under control, profits are recurring, and
cash generation supports the reported earnings.

## 6.8 Exceptional / One-Off Items

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** No exceptional items are assumed. In real analysis, distinguish ordinary start-up expenses from genuinely exceptional items and do not exclude recurring costs merely to improve adjusted earnings.


**Formula:**
`Underlying Profit ≈ Reported Profit − After-Tax One-Off Gains + After-Tax One-Off Losses`

Exceptional or one-off items are unusual gains or losses that can
distort reported profit. ELI5: if a company normally earns ₹100 but
sells an asset and records a one-time ₹50 gain, reported profit may
temporarily look like ₹150. Investors should separate recurring earning
power from temporary effects.

# 7. Per-Share & Shareholder Distribution Metrics

## 7.1 Diluted EPS

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Not calculable: no share count or dilutive instruments are provided. A private-company example also has no quoted share price for market-based per-share comparisons.


**Formula:**
`Diluted EPS = Adjusted Profit Attributable to Ordinary Shareholders / Diluted Weighted-Average Shares`

Diluted EPS considers potential ordinary shares that could arise from
options, convertible securities or other instruments. ELI5: if there
could be more shares in the future, existing profit would be divided
among more shares, potentially reducing EPS. It gives a more
conservative per-share earnings view when dilution is material.

## 7.2 Dividend Per Share \[DPS\]

**Formula:**
`DPS = Total Dividend Attributable to Ordinary Shares / Number of Ordinary Shares`

Dividend Per Share tells you how much dividend is paid or proposed for
each ordinary share. ELI5: if ₹10 crore is distributed to shareholders
and there are 10 crore shares, DPS is ₹1 per share.

## 7.3 Dividend Payout Ratio

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Not applicable because no dividend is paid and the example reports a loss. A payout ratio with negative earnings is generally not meaningful.


**Formula:** `Dividend Payout Ratio = Dividends / Net Profit × 100`

The Dividend Payout Ratio shows what percentage of profits is
distributed to shareholders. ELI5: if the company earns ₹100 and pays
₹30 as dividends, its payout ratio is 30%; the remaining ₹70 is
retained, subject to other equity movements.

## 7.4 Retained Earnings

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Under the simplified no-tax assumption and with no prior retained earnings, dividends or other equity movements, the quarter's loss reduces equity by ₹4,78,333. The formal retained-earnings balance must reflect all actual prior-period and current-period movements.


**Formula:**
`Retained Earnings = Opening Retained Earnings + Net Profit − Dividends ± Other Adjustments`

Retained Earnings are accumulated profits not distributed to
shareholders. ELI5: instead of giving all profits back to owners, the
company keeps some inside the business to fund growth, repay debt or
strengthen the balance sheet.

# 8. Valuation

## 8.1 Market Capitalisation \[Market Cap\]

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Not available because Nexora is hypothetical and privately held in this case; there is no market quote or market capitalisation supplied.


**Formula:** `Market Capitalisation = Share Price × Shares Outstanding`

Market Capitalisation is the market value of the company’s ordinary
equity. ELI5: if there are 10 crore shares at ₹100 each, the equity
market value is ₹1,000 crore. Market Cap is what investors currently
value the equity at, not its accounting book value.

## 8.2 Enterprise Value \[EV\]

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Not calculable without equity market value and a complete debt/cash/investment definition. Book cash and share capital are not substitutes for market capitalisation.


**Formula:**
`EV ≈ Market Capitalisation + Total Debt − Cash & Cash Equivalents`

Enterprise Value approximates the value of the operating business
available to all capital providers. ELI5: if you bought the whole
business, you would effectively take on its debt but also obtain its
cash, so EV adjusts Market Cap for these items. Detailed calculations
may also consider leases, non-operating investments and other debt-like
items.

## 8.3 P/E Ratio

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Not calculable because there is no share price or EPS; earnings are negative in the illustrative quarter in any case.


**Formula:** `P/E = Share Price / EPS`

The Price-to-Earnings ratio tells you how much investors are paying for
each rupee of earnings. ELI5: a ₹200 share with ₹10 EPS has a 20× P/E. A
high P/E can reflect high expected growth or quality; a low P/E can
reflect lower expectations, higher risk or undervaluation.

## 8.4 P/B Ratio

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Not calculable because no market value of equity or shares outstanding is given.


**Formula:** `P/B = Share Price / Book Value Per Share`

The Price-to-Book ratio compares market price with accounting book value
per share. ELI5: if BVPS is ₹100 and the share trades at ₹200, P/B is
2×. It is especially useful for banks, financial institutions and
asset-heavy companies, although accounting values do not always equal
economic value.

## 8.5 EV/EBITDA

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Not calculable because enterprise value is unavailable and EBITDA is negative. A negative denominator makes conventional valuation-multiple interpretation unreliable.


**Formula:** `EV/EBITDA = Enterprise Value / EBITDA`

EV/EBITDA compares the value of the operating business with its EBITDA.
ELI5: it asks how many times annual operating earnings investors are
effectively paying for the business. It is useful across companies with
different debt levels, but it does not account for Capex, working
capital, taxes or other cash costs.

## 8.6 Price-to-Sales \[P/S\]

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Not calculable because there is no market capitalisation or share price. Quarterly revenue alone does not establish a valuation multiple.


**Formula:** `P/S = Market Capitalisation / Revenue`

Price-to-Sales compares equity market value with revenue. ELI5: it tells
you how much investors are paying for every ₹1 of annual sales. It is
useful when earnings are temporarily low or negative, but businesses
with identical sales can have very different profitability, so P/S
should not be used alone.

## 8.7 Free Cash Flow Yield

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Not calculable because market capitalisation is unavailable and the defined FCF is negative ₹4,30,000. Do not present a yield without a meaningful market-value denominator.


**Formula:** `FCF Yield = Free Cash Flow / Market Capitalisation × 100`

FCF Yield measures free cash flow relative to the company’s market
value. ELI5: if a company is valued at ₹1,000 crore and generates ₹50
crore of FCF, its FCF yield is 5%. A higher yield can indicate a cheaper
valuation, but the sustainability and growth of FCF matter.

# 9. The Complete Analysis Chain

## 9.1 Profitability Chain

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** ₹8.50 lakh revenue → ₹4.50 lakh EBITDA loss → ₹4.7833 lakh EBIT/PBT loss under the simplified assumptions. The example makes visible how payroll and operating costs exceed revenue in a first quarter.


**Formula:** `Sales → Expenses → EBITDA → EBIT → PBT → PAT → EPS`

The profitability chain shows how revenue gradually becomes earnings
attributable to shareholders. EBITDA is an intermediate, accounting-based operating measure—not cash flow and not the accounting bottom line; PAT is the bottom-line profit measure, while CFO and FCF show cash generation. ELI5: start with money coming in, subtract
the costs of running the business, recognise asset usage, financing and
taxes, and eventually arrive at profit per share. It helps identify
exactly where profitability is improving or deteriorating.

## 9.2 Cash-Generation Chain

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** ₹4.7833 lakh pre-tax loss → ₹1.10 lakh CFO → ₹5.40 lakh physical plus software capital investment → ₹4.30 lakh negative FCF. Financing cash inflow of ₹20 lakh then explains why closing cash is still ₹14.70 lakh.


**Formula:** `Net Profit → Operating Cash Flow → Capex → Free Cash Flow`

The cash-generation chain shows how accounting earnings become usable
cash. ELI5: a company can report profit, but it still needs to collect
customers’ payments and fund inventory; after those cash effects and
capital spending, the remaining cash is FCF.

## 9.3 Return Chain

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** The business is too new for stable ROE, ROCE, ROA or asset-turnover conclusions. The right lesson is to obtain comparable periods and use average balance-sheet denominators, not to manufacture a return metric from incomplete data.


**Formula:** `ROE + ROCE + ROA + Asset Turnover`

The return chain evaluates how efficiently management converts capital
and assets into profits. ELI5: two companies can earn the same profit
but require very different amounts of capital to do it. These measures
reveal that difference.

## 9.4 Growth Chain

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** No growth chain can be calculated from a single quarter. Use this quarter as the baseline for future revenue, EBITDA, EBIT, PAT, EPS and FCF comparisons once consistent subsequent-period data exist.


**Formula:**
`Revenue Growth → EBITDA Growth → EBIT Growth → PAT Growth → EPS Growth → FCF Growth`

The growth chain checks whether growth reaches the shareholder’s bottom
line and cash generation. ELI5: sales growth is good, but ideally
operating profit, net profit, EPS and FCF should also grow sustainably.
If sales grow rapidly while EPS or FCF stagnates, the quality of growth
deserves investigation.

## 9.5 Valuation Chain

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Market cap, EV and valuation multiples cannot be calculated from this private-company teaching case. The financial statements explain operating economics; a valuation needs a market value or an explicit valuation model and forecast assumptions.


**Formula:** `Market Cap → EV → P/E → P/B → EV/EBITDA → P/S → FCF Yield`

The valuation chain asks what investors are paying for earnings, assets,
operating profit, sales and free cash flow. ELI5: a great company can
still be a poor investment if its share price is far above what its
future economics justify. Valuation must therefore be considered
alongside growth, profitability, balance-sheet strength and earnings
quality.

## 9.6 Investor’s Master Question

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** The quarter teaches the central question: revenue is ₹8.50 lakh, EBITDA is negative ₹4.50 lakh, CFO is positive ₹1.10 lakh due in part to advances, FCF is negative ₹4.30 lakh after investment, and cash is ₹14.70 lakh because of founder funding. Each number tells a different part of the story.


**Formula:**
`Business Quality + Growth + Cash Generation + Returns + Balance-Sheet Strength + Valuation`

The purpose of these metrics is not to memorise ratios but to answer: Is
this a good business, is it improving, does it generate real cash, does
it use capital efficiently, is its balance sheet safe, and is the share
price reasonable relative to those qualities? ELI5: first understand how
the business makes money, then check whether profit becomes cash, then
check capital efficiency and financial strength, and only then judge the
stock price.

# 10. Software & IT Company Analysis

This chapter adds metrics used to analyse software and technology businesses. Not every metric applies to every company. Traditional IT services firms, software product companies, and Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) businesses have different revenue models and cost structures. Use only metrics that fit the company's business model and are disclosed or can be calculated reliably.

## 10.1 Identify the Software Business Model

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Nexora is a hybrid business in the case: software development/consulting services plus proprietary annual subscriptions. Analyse services revenue separately from subscription revenue because delivery economics and recognition timing differ.


**Analytical method:** Classify revenue into the relevant business model(s), using company disclosures.

- **IT services and consulting:** Revenue is earned by providing people, expertise and technology delivery to clients.
- **Software products and licences:** Revenue comes from selling or licensing software, often with maintenance and support.
- **SaaS / cloud subscriptions:** Customers pay periodically to access hosted software.
- **Hybrid model:** The company earns from a combination of services, licences, subscriptions, platforms and support.

ELI5: Two companies may both be called software companies, but one sells skilled people's time, another sells a software product, and a third sells ongoing access to a cloud application. Their economics and the most useful ratios are different. First identify how the company earns money.

## 10.21 Capex and Opex in Software Companies

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Physical equipment spend is ₹2,40,000; capitalised software development cash spend is ₹3,00,000; recurring software tools are paid at ₹1,20,000 but only ₹30,000 is expensed for the quarter because three months were consumed; research and marketing are expensed under the assumptions. Cash outflow and current-period expense are not interchangeable.


**Opex (operating expenditure)** is spending recognised as an expense to run and deliver the business, such as employee salaries, routine cloud hosting, rent, sales and marketing, support, and research or development costs that do not qualify for capitalisation. **Capex (capital expenditure)** is cash spent acquiring or creating qualifying long-lived assets, such as servers and equipment, and certain software or development costs where the applicable accounting requirements are met. Classification depends on the facts, contract terms and accounting standards; not every software-development cost or cloud bill is Capex.

Examples:
- **Often Opex:** employee compensation, routine AWS/Azure/cloud consumption, office costs, marketing, customer support, maintenance and development research that is expensed.
- **Potentially Capex:** servers or equipment owned by the company; qualifying purchased software or licences recognised as intangible assets; and development costs capitalised only when the relevant recognition criteria are satisfied.

**Why investors should care:** Expensed development reduces current-period reported profit immediately. Capitalised development is initially recorded as an asset, with amortisation and possible impairment recognised later. Capitalisation can therefore increase current-period reported EBITDA or profit compared with immediate expensing, depending on the presentation and metric; it does not eliminate the cash outflow. For cash-flow analysis, examine actual cash spent, CFO, Capex and FCF, and read the company's accounting policy.

ELI5: Opex is the cost of running the software business today; Capex is spending on qualifying assets intended to benefit the business over time. A company can report strong EBITDA while using substantial cash to build software or infrastructure.

## 10.22 Expensed vs Capitalised Development: Worked Example

Use the actual figures from the Nexora accounting companion. During May 2026, Nexora pays **₹3,00,000** for development expenditure that the example assumes meets the applicable capitalisation criteria. Separately, it spends **₹1,00,000 on product research**, which is expensed. The ₹3,00,000 treatment is an assumption for teaching; a real company must demonstrate that the criteria are met.

### Actual illustrative treatment in the Nexora case: capitalise qualifying development

**Initial journal entry — May 2026**

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---:|---:|
| Capitalised Software Development | ₹3,00,000 | — |
| Bank | — | ₹3,00,000 |

The software is assumed to become available for use on 1 June 2026, with a 36-month useful life and no residual value. June amortisation is ₹3,00,000 ÷ 36 = **₹8,333.33**.

| Impact for the quarter | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Development cash paid | ₹3,00,000 |
| Development cost capitalised | ₹3,00,000 |
| Current-quarter amortisation | ₹8,333.33 |
| Net software asset at 30 June | ₹2,91,666.67 |
| EBITDA loss for the quarter | ₹4,50,000 |
| EBIT / pre-tax loss for the quarter | ₹4,78,333.33 |

The ₹1,00,000 product research cost remains a current-period expense in this example. Research spending is not capitalised simply because it relates to a future product.

### Counterfactual teaching scenario: expense the ₹3,00,000 immediately

This is a **comparison only**, not a claim that Nexora may freely choose either treatment. If the facts and applicable standards require immediate expensing, the ₹3,00,000 is recorded as an expense rather than an intangible asset. In that scenario, no software asset is recognised for this expenditure and the related ₹8,333 amortisation is not recorded.

| Measure | Capitalise qualifying development (case treatment) | Expense development immediately (counterfactual) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Total revenue | ₹8,50,000 | ₹8,50,000 |
| Operating expenses before D&A, including development treatment | ₹13,00,000 | ₹16,00,000 |
| **EBITDA** | **−₹4,50,000** | **−₹7,50,000** |
| Equipment depreciation | ₹20,000 | ₹20,000 |
| Software amortisation | ₹8,333 | ₹0 |
| **EBIT / pre-tax loss** | **−₹4,78,333** | **−₹7,70,000** |
| Software asset, net at 30 June | ₹2,91,667 | ₹0 for this expenditure |
| Cash spent on development | ₹3,00,000 | ₹3,00,000 |

**What changed?** Under the capitalisation assumption, current-quarter EBITDA is ₹3,00,000 higher because the development cost is not an immediate operating expense. EBIT is ₹2,91,667 higher because the capitalised treatment includes ₹8,333 amortisation, while immediate expensing includes the full ₹3,00,000 expense and no related amortisation. Cash spent is identical. Capitalisation changes expense timing and asset presentation; it does not create cash.

### How the same spending affects cash flow

Under the classifications used in the accounting companion, capitalised software development is an investing outflow. If the same ₹3,00,000 were expensed and classified as operating cash flow, the indirect cash-flow reconciliation would change accordingly.

| Cash-flow measure | Capitalise qualifying development | Expense development immediately |
|---|---:|---:|
| Operating cash flow (CFO) | ₹1,10,000 | −₹1,90,000 |
| Equipment Capex | −₹2,40,000 | −₹2,40,000 |
| Capitalised development investing outflow | −₹3,00,000 | ₹0 |
| Refundable deposit (classified as investing in the example) | −₹1,00,000 | −₹1,00,000 |
| **Net increase in cash** | **₹14,70,000** | **₹14,70,000** |
| **FCF after equipment and all development spending** | **−₹4,30,000** | **−₹4,30,000** |

The operating/investing split changes, but total cash does not. For the capitalisation case, the stated FCF is CFO less physical Capex and capitalised development cash outflow. In the immediate-expensing case, development cash has already reduced CFO, so deduct physical Capex only; do not deduct the development cash outflow a second time. Otherwise, an analyst can double-count the same spending or make a company that capitalises development appear to generate more FCF simply because the outflow sits outside CFO.

**Investor takeaway:** Determine whether capitalisation is required by the applicable standard and supported by evidence. Then examine the amount capitalised, the period capitalisation begins, the available-for-use date, amortisation, impairment risk and cash spent. Neither capitalisation nor immediate expensing is an unrestricted presentation choice.

## 10.23 Cash Flow Analysis and Potential Red Flags

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** CFO is positive ₹1,10,000 despite a loss because cash was received ahead of service delivery. However, FCF is negative ₹4,30,000 after equipment and software investment. The contract-liability balance and future delivery costs must be considered; customer prepayments are not free profit.


High EBITDA alongside persistently weak CFO or FCF is a reason to investigate, not automatic proof of poor accounting or a weak business. Review several years, the notes to accounts, accounting policies, auditor observations, business model and management explanation.

### Potential issues to investigate

1. **Development capitalisation lifts current earnings.** If a growing share of development expenditure is capitalised, current EBITDA and profit may look stronger than if the same eligible costs had been expensed immediately. Compare capitalised development with revenue, total development spend and peers; investigate changes in policy or application. Capitalisation is appropriate when recognition criteria are met and is not inherently improper.
2. **Slow customer collections.** Revenue may be recognised before cash is collected. Receivables, contract assets or unbilled revenue growing faster than revenue, rising Days Sales Outstanding (DSO), overdue balances or write-offs can indicate collection risk or weaker customer terms. Review ageing and expected credit loss provisions.
3. **Substantial recurring reinvestment.** Product upgrades, cybersecurity, cloud infrastructure, AI and technical talent can require significant cash. Strong EBITDA may not translate into much FCF after necessary investment. Examine both expensed and capitalised development spending, physical Capex and FCF after all material investment.
4. **Capitalised assets may not earn expected returns.** A product may underperform, become obsolete or lose customers. The company may then need to recognise impairment, reducing future reported profit. Track intangible asset balances, amortisation periods, impairment charges and commercial adoption.
5. **Temporary cash-flow support from payables.** Delaying supplier payments can temporarily increase CFO. Check whether payables and payment days are rising unusually, whether supplier terms are sustainable, and whether cash-flow improvement comes from better collections or delayed payments.
6. **FCF definition omits material investment.** A headline FCF measure may deduct physical Capex but omit material capitalised software development outflows. Reconcile the company's measure to the cash-flow statement and use a consistent definition across peers.
7. **Non-cash expenses and dilution need context.** Non-cash charges can be added back in the indirect CFO reconciliation, but some still represent economic costs. For example, share-based compensation may dilute shareholders even though it is not a cash payment in the period. Review dilution, recurring “one-off” adjustments, taxes, interest and acquisition-related cash outflows.

### Software-company cash-generation checklist

- Compare EBITDA, EBIT and PAT growth over three to five years.
- Compare CFO with PAT and EBITDA, investigating persistent gaps and unusual working-capital movements.
- Review receivables, DSO, unbilled revenue, contract assets, payables and payment days.
- Combine expensed and capitalised development spending to understand total investment.
- Track capitalised software assets, amortisation, useful-life assumptions and impairment.
- Calculate FCF consistently after physical Capex and material capitalised development cash outflows.
- Check whether cash generation improves as the business scales, without depending on temporary working-capital benefits.

**Interpretation:** A company may have weak FCF because it is investing heavily in a promising product, because customers pay slowly, because margins are deteriorating, or because reported earnings do not convert well into cash. These situations have different implications. Determine which explanation fits the evidence before forming an investment conclusion.


## 10.24 Standards and Recognition Criteria for Capitalising Development Costs

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** The ₹3,00,000 capitalisation is a stipulated assumption, not proof that the criteria have been met. Under Ind AS 38 where applicable, maintain evidence for technical feasibility, intention and ability to complete/use/sell, probable future benefits, adequate resources and reliable measurement of attributable costs. Research-phase spending is expensed; qualifying development costs are capitalised only from the date all criteria are met.


For an Indian company reporting under Indian Accounting Standards, **Ind AS 38 — Intangible Assets** is the principal standard for internally generated software and other intangible assets. IAS 38 is the corresponding international standard. The company must apply the accounting framework applicable to it; do not assume every company follows Ind AS.

**Core rule:** Under Ind AS 38 paragraph 57, development expenditure is recognised as an intangible asset only when the company can demonstrate all six criteria below. A project budget, management approval or expectation of future revenue is not sufficient on its own.

| Recognition criterion | ELI5 meaning | Examples of supporting evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Technical feasibility of completion | The company can complete the software so it will be available for use or sale. | Technical architecture, prototype results, test evidence, engineering assessment. |
| 2. Intention to complete and use or sell | Management is committed to finishing the asset. | Approved product roadmap, project authorisation and documented decisions. |
| 3. Ability to use or sell | The company can put the software to use or commercialise it. | Deployment plan, sales strategy, relevant ownership or contractual rights. |
| 4. Probable future economic benefits | There is a credible basis for expecting benefits, such as revenue or cost savings. | Market research, customer demand, approved business case or operational benefits analysis. |
| 5. Adequate technical, financial and other resources | The company has the people, funding and resources needed to complete and use or sell it. | Funding approval, staffing plan, committed budget and delivery plan. |
| 6. Reliable measurement of attributable expenditure | The company can identify and measure the costs attributable to the asset reliably. | Project-level time records, payroll allocation, vendor invoices, project codes and ledger reconciliation. |

**All six criteria must be demonstrated.** If the company cannot distinguish the research phase from the development phase of an internal project, the expenditure is treated as research expenditure and expensed.

### When capitalisation begins and ends

- **Research phase:** Expenditure is expensed as incurred. Research includes activities intended to obtain new knowledge, explore alternatives or evaluate possible applications before a qualifying development project is established.
- **Development phase before the criteria are met:** Expense the expenditure. Costs already recognised as expenses cannot later be reinstated as part of the asset merely because the project subsequently becomes viable.
- **From the date all criteria are first met:** Capitalise only qualifying expenditure that is directly attributable to creating and preparing the asset for its intended use or sale, and only when its cost can be measured reliably.
- **When the asset is available for intended use:** Stop adding development costs to the asset for that completed stage. Amortisation generally begins when the asset is available for use. Subsequent maintenance and upgrades must be assessed separately; later expenditure is not automatically capitalisable.

### Employee salaries and shared project work

Directly attributable employee-benefit costs can form part of the cost of a qualifying intangible asset. Where employees work across several projects or activities, the company should use a reasonable, consistent and supportable allocation method. It should allocate the relevant salary cost to the work actually performed, then assess each activity against the recognition criteria.

For example, an engineer's monthly salary is ₹1,20,000. Reliable time records indicate ₹20,000 relates to research, ₹40,000 to qualifying development after all criteria are met, ₹30,000 to customer delivery, ₹20,000 to routine support and ₹10,000 to general administration and training. In this illustration, ₹40,000 may qualify for capitalisation if it is directly attributable and all requirements are met; the other allocations are generally expensed or accounted for under other applicable requirements. The percentages and amounts are illustrative, not prescribed by the standard.

The project name alone does not determine treatment. A company-owned product project can include research and maintenance that must be expensed; work on a customer project is usually a service-delivery cost, although separate requirements for contract costs or other assets may apply to particular facts.

### Costs generally excluded from an internally generated intangible asset

Ind AS 38 identifies categories that are not included in the cost of an internally generated intangible asset, including research expenditure, general administrative and selling costs that cannot be directly attributed to preparing the asset, training costs, identified inefficiencies and initial operating losses. Apply the detailed requirements of the standard to the specific facts rather than relying solely on this summary.

### Evidence and controls an investor can look for

A well-controlled company would ordinarily be able to explain and substantiate:

1. The project scope, intended use or market, and asset ownership or relevant rights.
2. The technical and commercial evidence supporting the six criteria.
3. The documented date on which all criteria were first met.
4. The basis for allocating employee time and other directly attributable costs.
5. Reconciliation of capitalised costs to payroll, invoices and the general ledger.
6. The date the asset became available for use, its useful life, amortisation method and impairment assessment.
7. The accounting policy and material judgements disclosed in the financial statements.

These are practical evidence and control recommendations, not a claim that the standard mandates one particular timesheet format or approval workflow.

### Investor red flags

Investigate further if capitalised development rises materially faster than revenue or total development spending; the company cannot explain its capitalisation commencement dates; time and payroll allocations are weakly supported; routine maintenance appears to be capitalised; assets accumulate without commercial adoption; or EBITDA improves while cash generation after development investment remains weak. None of these observations alone proves an accounting breach, but each warrants review of the accounting policy, notes, audit report and multi-year trends.

**ELI5:** Capitalisation is permitted only when the company can show that it has moved beyond research into a feasible, resourced and economically justified development project, and can reliably measure the costs that directly belong to that project. It is not a discretionary way to make salaries disappear from current expenses.

# 11. Debt, Liquidity and Financial Resilience

Debt and liquidity should be assessed even for software companies that historically operate with low borrowing. Acquisitions, leases, buybacks, changing cash balances and weaker demand can alter the risk profile. Use consistent definitions and assess trends rather than relying on one ratio.

## 11.1 Debt-to-Equity

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** No interest-bearing debt is included, so the illustrative ratio would be zero only under the case assumptions and a stated debt definition. Actual debt-to-equity must consider borrowings and potentially lease liabilities, and should not imply financial strength merely because debt was omitted from a teaching case.


**Formula:** `Debt-to-Equity = Interest-Bearing Debt / Shareholders' Equity`

ELI5: This compares lender funding with shareholder accounting capital. A high ratio can indicate greater financial leverage. If equity is zero or negative, the ratio is not meaningfully interpretable in the usual way. State whether lease liabilities are included.

## 11.2 Debt-to-Assets

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** No borrowing balance is supplied. A ratio based on zero debt would be a model artefact rather than an investment conclusion.


**Formula:** `Debt-to-Assets % = Interest-Bearing Debt / Total Assets × 100`

ELI5: This shows the share of the asset base financed by interest-bearing debt. It is not the same as total liabilities divided by total assets, because trade payables and other liabilities are not necessarily borrowings.

## 11.3 Net Debt to EBITDA

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Not meaningful because there is no debt in the model and EBITDA is negative ₹4,50,000. Negative EBITDA makes this leverage multiple unsuitable for conventional interpretation.


**Formula:** `Net Debt / EBITDA = (Interest-Bearing Debt − Eligible Cash and Cash Equivalents) / EBITDA`

ELI5: This compares net borrowings with annual EBITDA. It is a rough leverage indicator, not a literal repayment timetable. EBITDA is not cash available for debt repayment, and a negative net debt figure means the company has more eligible cash than debt. If EBITDA is zero or negative, the ratio is generally not useful.

## 11.4 Interest Coverage Ratio

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Not calculable in a meaningful way because no interest expense or borrowing is included and EBIT is negative.


**Formula:** `Interest Coverage = EBIT / Finance Cost`

ELI5: If EBIT is ₹200 crore and finance cost is ₹40 crore, interest coverage is 5×. Operating profit is five times the reported finance cost. Lower coverage can signal vulnerability, but check what finance cost includes and whether EBIT contains one-off items. If EBIT is negative, coverage does not indicate a safe ability to pay interest.

## 11.5 EBITDA Interest Coverage

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Not calculable because the case assumes no interest expense; a zero denominator in the coverage ratio would make the metric undefined, not infinitely safe.


**Formula:** `EBITDA Interest Coverage = EBITDA / Finance Cost`

ELI5: This compares pre-depreciation operating earnings with finance cost. It will normally be higher than EBIT-based coverage when depreciation and amortisation are material. EBITDA coverage does not account for the cash needed to replace assets, taxes, working capital or loan principal, so it should not replace EBIT coverage or cash-flow analysis.

## 11.6 Debt Service Coverage Ratio

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Not calculable because scheduled principal and interest payments are not provided.


**Formula:** `DSCR = Cash Flow Available for Debt Service / (Scheduled Principal Repayments + Interest Due)`

The exact definition of cash flow available for debt service varies by lender, agreement and analytical convention; document the selected definition.

ELI5: Interest is the charge for borrowing; principal is the amount borrowed that must be repaid. DSCR asks whether the company can generate enough relevant cash to cover both. A company can cover interest yet still struggle to repay a large principal instalment.

## 11.7 Current Ratio, Quick Ratio and Cash Ratio

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Using current assets ₹17,60,000 and current contract liabilities ₹8,50,000, a broad current ratio proxy is **2.07×**. Excluding prepaid expenses, the quick ratio is (₹14,70,000 cash + ₹2,00,000 receivables) ÷ ₹8,50,000 = **1.96×**. The cash ratio is ₹14,70,000 ÷ ₹8,50,000 = **1.73×**. These ratios rely on the stated classifications and a very short, simplified opening quarter.


**Formulas:**
- `Current Ratio = Current Assets / Current Liabilities`
- `Quick Ratio = (Current Assets − Inventory − Prepayments) / Current Liabilities`
- `Cash Ratio = Cash and Cash Equivalents / Current Liabilities`

ELI5: The current ratio considers all current assets; the quick ratio removes less-liquid inventory and prepayments; the cash ratio focuses on cash and cash equivalents. For software companies with little inventory, the current and quick ratios may be closer. Review the quality and accessibility of cash, including restricted balances and obligations.

## 11.8 Debt Maturity and Refinancing Risk

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** No debt maturity schedule is supplied. A real analysis must inspect contractual maturity dates, covenants, interest-rate reset terms and available liquidity.


**Analytical method:** Map principal repayments, lease commitments and other material financing obligations by due period; compare them with cash, expected operating cash flow and available facilities.

ELI5: A company may have manageable total debt but still face a problem if a large amount falls due at once. A maturity schedule shows when cash will be needed and whether the company might have to refinance at less favourable rates.

## 11.9 Contingent Liabilities and Guarantees

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** No guarantees, litigation, tax disputes or other contingencies are included. Their absence from this teaching case does not mean they are absent in an actual company.


**Analytical method:** Review disclosed guarantees, tax disputes, legal claims, contractual commitments and other contingencies. Compare their nature and potential size with equity, cash flow and available liquidity.

ELI5: A contingent liability is a possible future obligation, not always a bill that must be paid today. It can nevertheless become important if an adverse outcome occurs. Do not treat all contingent liabilities as certain debt; assess likelihood, size and disclosure.

# 12. Integrated Software Company Analysis

## 12.1 Three-Statement Linkage

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** The ₹4,78,333 pre-tax loss reduces equity; depreciation and amortisation reduce profit without current-period cash payments; equipment and software investment use cash and create assets; customer receipts create cash and contract liabilities; collecting ₹6,00,000 from Customer A increases cash and reduces receivables without creating new revenue.


**Analysis chain:** `P&L → Balance Sheet → Cash Flow Statement → Back to Balance Sheet`

Key links include:
- PAT generally flows into retained earnings, subject to dividends and other equity movements.
- Depreciation and amortisation reduce accounting profit but are generally added back in the indirect operating cash-flow reconciliation.
- Capex uses cash and normally adds to property, equipment or qualifying intangible assets.
- Receivables and contract assets can rise when revenue is recognised before cash is collected.
- Deferred revenue can rise when cash is received or billed before revenue is recognised, subject to the applicable accounting rules.
- Borrowing proceeds increase cash and debt; principal repayment reduces cash and debt.
- Share issuance can increase cash and equity, while buybacks and dividends use cash.

ELI5: The three statements are connected views of the same business. Profit explains performance, the balance sheet shows what the company owns and owes at a point in time, and cash flow explains where cash came from and where it went.

## 12.2 Earnings Quality and Cash Conversion

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Positive CFO of ₹1,10,000 should be interpreted alongside a ₹4,78,333 loss and ₹8,50,000 contract liabilities. The cash result is supported by customer prepayments, while FCF remains negative after investment. This is why profit, CFO, FCF and deferred revenue must be reviewed together.


**Formulas:**
- `CFO / PAT = Operating Cash Flow / PAT`
- `FCF / PAT = (Operating Cash Flow − Capital Expenditure) / PAT`
- `Receivable Days = Average Trade Receivables / Credit Sales × 365`, where credit sales are available or reasonably approximated and the limitation is stated.

ELI5: If profits rise but cash flow does not, investigate collections, contract assets, working capital, tax timing and non-cash or one-off items. One weak year can reflect timing, while a repeated gap may be a warning sign. Ratios can be distorted when PAT is near zero or negative.

## 12.3 Peer and Trend Comparison

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** One quarter cannot support peer ranking or trend claims. Use Nexora as a baseline, then compare several periods with companies of the same business model, consistent accounting policies and matching metric definitions.


**Method:** Compare several years of company performance, where available, with relevant peers using consistent definitions and periods.

For IT services, consider revenue growth, constant-currency growth, operating margin, utilisation, attrition, revenue per employee, cash conversion and client concentration. For SaaS, consider ARR growth, NRR, churn, gross margin, CAC payback, Rule of 40 and free cash flow. For software products, consider recurring revenue, licence mix, R&D intensity, product margins and renewal economics.

ELI5: A ratio is more useful when you know whether it is improving and how it compares with similar companies. Comparing a SaaS company directly with a labour-intensive IT services company can produce misleading conclusions.

## 12.4 Sector-Specific Metric Selection

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Separate services from subscriptions. Services analysis needs delivery margin, utilisation and employee productivity; subscriptions need recurring revenue, renewal, retention, churn and gross margin. The example does not provide enough operating data to calculate most of those KPIs.


Use the metrics appropriate to the business model:
- **IT services and consulting:** Constant-currency growth, utilisation, attrition, employee cost ratio, revenue per employee, onsite/offshore mix, deal conversion, client concentration and operating margin.
- **SaaS and subscription software:** ARR/MRR, NRR/GRR, churn, CAC, LTV, CAC payback, gross margin, deferred revenue/RPO and Rule of 40.
- **Software products and platforms:** Recurring revenue, licence and maintenance mix, product gross margin, R&D intensity, capitalised development, renewals, product concentration and acquisition economics.
- **Hybrid companies:** Analyse the business lines separately where disclosures permit, then reconcile them to consolidated results.

ELI5: Do not force one set of ratios onto every technology company. Choose measures that explain how that specific company earns revenue, incurs costs and retains customers.

## 12.5 Software Company Red-Flag Checklist

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** The quarter is not a fraud or distress case; it is a deliberately simplified start-up illustration. It does show why positive CFO can coexist with a loss, why capitalised development needs evidence, and why contract liabilities imply future delivery obligations.


Investigate further when several of these appear together:
- Revenue growth slows while employee costs or headcount rise materially.
- Reported rupee growth is strong but constant-currency growth is weak.
- Deal wins increase but subsequent revenue growth or conversion remains weak.
- Receivables or contract assets grow persistently faster than revenue.
- Operating margins decline without a clear explanation from investment, wage or business-mix changes.
- PAT grows but operating cash flow remains weak for multiple periods.
- A SaaS company has deteriorating NRR, rising churn or lengthening CAC payback.
- R&D capitalisation rises materially and reported earnings become increasingly dependent on capitalisation assumptions.
- A large share of revenue depends on one customer, sector, geography or product.
- Acquisitions increase reported growth but incremental returns and cash generation deteriorate.
- Debt, lease commitments or near-term maturities rise while cash generation weakens.
- Management highlights AI or large deal announcements without measurable evidence of revenue, margins or cash conversion.

ELI5: One warning sign does not prove a company is bad. Several warning signs appearing together, persisting over time and lacking a credible explanation deserve deeper investigation.

## 12.6 Investor's Software Analysis Checklist

> **Nexora worked-example connection:** Available facts: ₹8.50 lakh revenue, ₹4.50 lakh EBITDA loss, ₹1.10 lakh CFO, ₹4.30 lakh negative FCF under the stated definition, ₹8.50 lakh contract liabilities and ₹14.70 lakh closing cash. Unavailable facts include growth, share-based metrics, valuation, employee KPIs, customer retention, tax and a complete statutory disclosure set.


Before forming an investment view, ask:
1. **Business model:** Is the company primarily an IT services provider, SaaS company, software product vendor or hybrid?
2. **Growth:** Is growth organic, sustainable and measured consistently in reported and constant-currency terms where relevant?
3. **Profitability:** Are operating and gross margins stable or improving for understandable reasons?
4. **People and delivery:** For IT services, what do utilisation, attrition, employee costs and revenue per employee indicate?
5. **Customer economics:** For SaaS, are recurring revenue, retention, churn and customer-acquisition economics healthy?
6. **Cash generation:** Does operating cash flow support reported profit, and does free cash flow remain healthy after necessary investment?
7. **Balance sheet:** Are debt, leases, contingencies and maturities manageable under weaker operating conditions?
8. **Competitive position:** Are products, relationships, expertise or platforms defensible, and how might AI or technology shifts change that position?
9. **Governance and accounting:** Are disclosures clear, related-party transactions reasonable, and auditor observations understood?
10. **Valuation:** What expectations are embedded in the share price, and what do bear, base and bull scenarios imply?
11. **Shareholder returns:** Are dividends, buybacks and acquisitions funded sustainably without undermining the business?
12. **Evidence quality:** Which conclusions rely on audited figures, company-reported operating metrics, estimates or assumptions?

ELI5: First understand how the company makes money. Then determine whether growth is real, customers remain, profits turn into cash, the balance sheet is safe and the business can adapt. Finally decide whether the current share price offers a reasonable return for the risks.

# Appendix A. Formula and Interpretation Conventions

1. **Use averages where appropriate.** Ratios that compare a period's revenue or profit with a balance-sheet amount should generally use average balances when opening and closing data are available.
2. **Define every denominator.** Receivable days should use credit sales where available; payable days should use credit purchases where available. If proxies are used, label them.
3. **Define debt consistently.** State whether lease liabilities are included and how cash equivalents, restricted cash and non-operating investments are treated.
4. **Keep coverage measures distinct.** EBIT interest coverage, EBITDA interest coverage, cash interest coverage and DSCR answer different questions.
5. **Handle negative denominators carefully.** Negative or near-zero earnings can make P/E, leverage, growth and return ratios meaningless or unstable. Explain the limitation instead of presenting a conventional interpretation.
6. **Distinguish percentages from percentage points.** A margin moving from 10% to 12% rises by 2 percentage points, or 20% relative to the original margin.
7. **Label periods correctly.** TTM means trailing twelve months. CAGR is a compounded annualised growth rate over a specified multi-year period. Neither should be confused with the latest quarter's year-on-year growth.
8. **Separate reported and adjusted values.** Label company-reported figures, analyst adjustments, estimates and assumptions distinctly.
9. **Avoid universal thresholds.** Healthy ratios vary by industry, maturity, business model, accounting policy and economic cycle.
10. **Avoid double-counting.** Keep enterprise-level measures (such as EV/EBITDA) consistent with enterprise value and equity-level measures (such as P/E) consistent with equity value.
11. **Use consolidated accounts when appropriate.** Review standalone accounts as supplementary information, especially where subsidiaries, acquisitions or overseas operations are material.
12. **Do not assume unavailable metrics.** If a company does not disclose utilisation, ARR, NRR, CAC or other operating data and the figures cannot be calculated reliably, mark them as unavailable rather than inventing estimates.
13. **Treat capitalised development consistently in cash analysis.** Check whether the stated FCF measure deducts cash spent on capitalised software development as well as physical Capex. CFO may differ depending on the accounting classification of development spending; when comparing companies, document the definition and avoid omitting material investment outflows.
14. **Check the applicable accounting framework for development costs.** For companies applying Ind AS, consult Ind AS 38 and document evidence for all six development-recognition criteria, the date capitalisation begins, directly attributable cost measurement, and the date the asset is available for use. Previously expensed expenditure cannot be reinstated as an asset simply because the project later qualifies.

# Appendix B. Important Limitations

This textbook is an educational framework, not a mechanical buy/sell system. The Nexora figures are hypothetical and are intended to teach the accounting-to-analysis chain; they are not forecasts, benchmarks, statutory accounts or evidence about an actual issuer. Ratios summarise selected aspects of a business and can be distorted by accounting classifications, acquisitions, seasonality, foreign exchange, unusual events and differences in company definitions. Financial statements and exchange disclosures should be read alongside management commentary, audit reports, competitive conditions and valuation assumptions. A strong company is not automatically an attractively priced stock, and a low valuation multiple is not automatically evidence of undervaluation.
